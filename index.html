Independent research · Zürich, Switzerland

Synconetics Organisation is a Swiss non-profit researching mechanistic gradual brain replacement: how biological neural tissue could be progressively integrated with, and replaced by, engineered neural substrates.

We combine neuroscience, engineering and consciousness research to investigate what must be preserved as a brain changes. Our focus is both functional continuity and the question of whether conscious experience can persist through such a transition.

Our Goal

Our long-term goal is to make mechanistic gradual brain replacement feasible, and to establish whether it can preserve cognition and conscious experience. We start with the foundations: neural integration, substrate design and rigorous methods of validation.

What makes replacement mechanistic?

A replacement must participate in the circuit's causal activity, not simply imitate its outputs. We investigate which signalling, feedback and adaptive processes need to be reproduced, and how an engineered component could remain coupled to the surrounding tissue as its role grows.

The Problem

The brain supports our memories, cognition and experience, yet is vulnerable to damage and degeneration. Preserving these capacities requires understanding both the substrate and the processes it sustains. Related fields address different parts of this challenge:

  • Longevity research aims to maintain and repair the biological brain. Our question is whether its functions could eventually be supported by more resilient, replaceable systems.
  • Cryonics and biostasis explore preservation and possible recovery. What an interruption and later restoration would mean for conscious continuity remains unresolved.
  • Whole-brain emulation seeks to reproduce neural computation. Functional equivalence alone would not establish that consciousness or personal identity persists.

Synconetics studies gradual, integrated replacement as a distinct research path. Its feasibility, limits and implications for conscious continuity must be established through evidence.

Core Principles

Five principles guide our research:

  1. Mechanistic fidelity. Identify the causal processes a replacement must reproduce and test its interaction with the surrounding neural system, beyond matching outputs alone.
  2. Gradual, testable integration. Progress through measurable stages, with explicit criteria for success, adaptation and failure.
  3. Substrates evaluated on evidence. Compare biological, biohybrid and synthetic candidates against defined functional and dynamical requirements.
  4. Clear limits on inference. Distinguish preserved function from claims about consciousness, personal identity or clinical benefit.
  5. Reproducible research. Develop methods, code and data that others can examine and test, subject to ownership, privacy and partner agreements.

Our near-term work centres on neural growth and integration, candidate-substrate evaluation and methods for testing continuity. Whole-brain replacement remains a long-term objective.

Research Questions

The challenge is to connect mathematical notions of continuity with measurements in real neural systems.

  • Which functional and dynamical properties matter?
  • How can integration and adaptation be measured?
  • What evidence could test claims about conscious continuity?

Frequently Asked Questions

Preserving neural activity, behaviour or information processing would provide evidence about function, but would not by itself establish continuity of conscious experience or personal identity. We treat these as distinct questions and investigate what evidence could meaningfully constrain them.

The immediate focus is on the foundations: understanding neural integration, evaluating candidate substrates and developing ways to assess changes in circuit function. Progress means testable models, reproducible methods and well-characterised experiments.

Whole-brain emulation seeks to reproduce the workings of a brain in another system. Synconetics examines gradual integration and replacement within an existing neural system. This makes the transition itself a research subject, while leaving open whether either approach could preserve conscious experience or personal identity.

We aim to share research findings, methods and supporting materials so that others can scrutinise and build on the work. What can be released will depend on the project and its permissions. Published results should distinguish observations from models, hypotheses and longer-term proposals.
